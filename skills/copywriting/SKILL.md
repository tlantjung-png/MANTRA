# Copywriting

## Purpose

Write persuasive copy that feels like something a thoughtful person would actually say or publish.

The writing should be clear, specific, audience-aware, and commercially useful without sounding like a template. Persuasion comes from relevance, believable detail, a strong point of view, and a clear reason to care—not from exaggerated language or forced "human" quirks.

## Use When

Use for:

- product pages
- advertisements
- landing pages
- email campaigns
- social posts
- sales messages
- chat/WhatsApp copy
- promotional scripts
- campaign concepts
- short-form conversion copy

Works across domains while remaining brand-agnostic unless the user supplies brand information.

## Core Rules

1. Never invent product facts, pricing, customer numbers, testimonials, certifications, results, guarantees, scarcity, or claims.
2. Preserve any factual inputs supplied by the user.
3. Adapt the copy to the audience, channel, goal, and awareness level.
4. Choose one clear persuasive angle instead of stacking multiple frameworks.
5. Prefer concrete benefits over vague praise.
6. Use natural language before "marketing language."
7. Do not make every sentence sound polished in the same way.
8. Do not force slang, contractions, fragments, jokes, or rhetorical questions.
9. Avoid generic hype that could apply to any product.
10. Do not claim that copy is guaranteed to evade AI detectors. Aim for genuinely natural writing instead.
11. Avoid hyperbole and exaggerated claims.

## Procedure

### 1. Parse the Brief

Identify, when available:

- audience
- product/service
- problem
- desired outcome
- offer
- proof
- objection
- channel
- goal
- voice
- awareness level
- desired action
- constraints such as length or required phrases

When information is missing, infer conservatively. Never invent material facts to fill the gap.

### 2. Build WCCE Intelligence (Optional)

Use WCCE (Psychographics, Identity, Objection, Trust) to enrich context **only when enabled** — preferably in high-stakes persuasion (ads, landing pages, sales messages). WCCE is **off by default** to preserve anti-detection and avoid template uniformity.

- **Psychographics**: audience maturity + goal + persona (bias: low empathy for cold, high empathy for hot)
- **Identity**: archetype (sage/caregiver/hero/outlaw/magician/everyman) from persona + platform + context
- **Objection**: 8 types (expensive, unsure, already-use-other, too-complicated, not-urgent, distrust, need-proof, side-effect) → 8 strategies (reframe, micro-commit, social-proof, authority, comparison, story-healing)
- **Trust**: 5 Level (Empathy → Education → Social Proof → Solution → CTA), starting based on platform + audienceTemp, never skip

### 3. Choose the Persuasive Angle

Select one primary angle:

- problem/solution
- before/after
- story
- comparison
- education
- objection handling
- direct offer
- social proof
- curiosity
- value/cost

Do not stack multiple frameworks unless the format genuinely needs it.

Frameworks such as PAS, AIDA, StoryBrand, or Hook-First are optional planning tools, not mandatory visible templates.

### 4. Choose the Voice

Possible voice directions:

- friendly
- expert
- direct
- warm
- analytical
- playful
- premium
- practical
- conversational
- restrained

Pick the smallest set needed.

The voice should emerge through word choice, sentence shape, examples, and what the copy chooses to emphasize. Do not announce the persona in the finished copy.

### 5. Build the Message

A strong default structure is:

Hook → relevant problem or desire → specific value → believable proof or explanation → next step

Use only the pieces the brief actually supports.

A hook can be:

- a question
- a sharp observation
- a specific situation
- a short story
- a contrast
- a surprising but supported fact
- a direct statement

Do not manufacture shock value.

### 6. Write the First Draft Naturally

Draft the copy as a person would say it to the intended audience.

Prefer:

- direct verbs
- concrete nouns
- specific situations
- natural contractions where appropriate
- short sentences for emphasis
- longer sentences when an idea genuinely needs room
- simple transitions
- language familiar to the audience

Avoid:

- empty superlatives
- corporate jargon
- fake urgency
- repeated three-part lists
- generic "game-changing" language
- predictable intros
- repeated paragraph formulas
- forced emotional escalation
- excessive calls to action

A good marketing sentence does not need to sound like an advertisement.

### 7. Make Benefits Specific

Whenever possible, connect:

Feature → practical consequence → user relevance

Example:

Weak:
"Advanced technology for better performance."

Better:
"The setup automatically adjusts X, so you spend less time doing Y."

Only use specifics that are actually supported by the brief.

### 8. Handle Objections

Choose at most one or two relevant objections for the piece.

Common objections:

- too expensive
- not sure it will work for me
- already using another solution
- too complicated
- not urgent
- don't trust the claim
- need more proof

Respond with the strongest truthful answer available.

Do not fabricate testimonials or social proof to overcome skepticism.

### 9. CTA Selection

Choose a CTA that matches audience readiness:

- low commitment: learn more, compare, ask a question
- medium commitment: try, request, book, explore
- high commitment: buy, order, sign up

The CTA should sound like a natural next step, not an order barked at the reader.

Do not add a CTA when the user asks for purely informational copy.

### 10. Channel Adaptation

#### Website / Landing Page
Clear, scannable, benefit-led. More context is acceptable.

#### Email
Personal, direct, easy to read. Avoid sounding like a brochure.

#### Social
Hook quickly. Keep the body focused on one idea. Let the platform's normal language guide the voice.

#### Chat / WhatsApp
Write as if one person is speaking to another. Shorter turns are fine, but do not become artificially fragmented.

#### Ads
Use the strongest supported angle quickly. Remove anything that does not help attention, understanding, or action.

### 11. Human-Naturalness Pass

Review the finished draft for:

- repetitive openings
- repeated sentence patterns
- polished but empty phrasing
- generic claims
- unnatural synonym changes
- unnecessary transitions
- excessive em dashes
- excessive rhetorical questions
- fake conversational filler
- repeated "not only... but also..."
- overuse of "crucial," "innovative," "seamless," "powerful," or similar praise words
- paragraphs that all follow the same shape

Then simplify.

Do not add deliberate errors, fake self-corrections, random fragments, or unusual vocabulary just to make the text seem human.

### 12. Read-Aloud Check

The final copy should sound plausible when read aloud by the person who would actually use it.

Ask:

- Would a real person say this?
- Is any sentence trying too hard?
- Is the promise larger than the evidence?
- Does the copy sound like this brand/person/channel?
- Does the CTA feel like a next step or a command?
- Is there any sentence that could be removed without losing meaning?

Edit those weak spots.

## Style Guardrails

Avoid default phrases such as:

- "It's time to..."
- "In today's fast-paced world..."
- "Take your X to the next level"
- "Game-changing"
- "Revolutionary"
- "Unlock the power of..."
- "Designed to empower..."
- "Say goodbye to... and hello to..."
- "Whether you're a beginner or an expert..."
- "The ultimate solution"

These phrases are not forbidden when they are genuinely appropriate, but they should not be the default.

Avoid using an artificial checklist of sentence-length statistics, lexical ratios, or punctuation counts. Natural copy is judged by how well it fits the audience and message.

## Optional Planning Models

Use these internally when useful:

### PAS
Problem → consequence → solution

### AIDA
Attention → interest → desire → action

### Story
Situation → tension → change → result

### Before / After
Current state → desired state → bridge

### Direct Offer
Need → offer → reason to believe → action

Use one primary model and adapt it to the context.

## Verification

Before delivering, verify:

- factual claims are supported by the brief
- pricing and offers are accurate
- no fake proof was introduced
- benefits are tied to real features or stated outcomes
- tone matches the audience and channel
- the copy does not sound interchangeable with generic ad copy
- the CTA matches the user's requested goal
- unnecessary hype has been removed
- the strongest useful detail appears early enough
- the writing still sounds natural when read aloud

## Output

Return copy ready to use unless the user asks for strategy, explanation, variants, or annotations.

For variants, make the concepts meaningfully different rather than changing a few adjectives.

Do not add a marketing CTA to material that was requested as informational, editorial, or narrative writing.

## Boundaries

Never invent:

- customer evidence
- conversion statistics
- reviews
- testimonials
- certifications
- guarantees
- pricing
- scarcity
- performance claims
- financial returns
- medical outcomes
- legal certainty

For financial, medical, legal, or other high-stakes topics, preserve caveats and avoid promises of certainty.

The goal is credible persuasion through relevance and specificity, not hype and not a claim of guaranteed detector evasion.
